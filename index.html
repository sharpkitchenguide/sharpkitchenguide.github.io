Why it happens

Cheap knives are usually soft, stamped steel

Many budget knives are stamped out of thin sheets of softer steel. Soft steel is easy to sharpen, but the edge rolls over as soon as it meets a cutting board.

Japanese-style knives usually use harder steel and a thinner edge angle. That's why they're known for staying sharp longer and gliding through food instead of pushing it.

How to choose

3 things a good everyday chef knife should have

  1. Hard, quality steel. It decides how long the edge lasts between sharpenings.
  2. A comfortable, balanced grip. You should be able to chop for ten minutes without your hand getting tired.
  3. An all-purpose shape. One knife that slices, dices and chops covers almost everything you cook.

Quick guide

Chef knife, santoku or gyuto?

The three shapes home cooks ask about most, and which one to start with.

Gyuto

The Japanese version of the Western chef knife: longer, pointed tip, curved belly for a rocking cut. Very versatile.

Santoku

Shorter, flatter blade with a rounded tip. Great for up-and-down chopping of vegetables, less suited to rocking.

FAQ

Common questions

What does the finger hole in the blade do?

It gives your index finger a place to rest, which helps you hold the knife steadily and control the cut, especially on harder vegetables.

Will a harder blade stay sharp forever?

No knife does. A harder blade usually holds its edge longer, but it still needs occasional honing or sharpening, and good habits (below) make a big difference.

3 habits that keep any knife sharp longer

  • Use a wood or plastic board. Glass, stone and ceramic boards dull an edge fast.
  • Wash it by hand and dry it right away. Dishwashers knock the edge against other items.
  • Store it in a block, on a magnetic strip or in a sheath, not loose in a drawer.

Always handle sharp knives with care, cut away from your body and keep them out of reach of children. Follow the maker's care instructions.
